Before you start

  • Any deck works — but a fresh Bicycle Rider Back deck (broken in for a day) handles best and is what every tutorial below uses.
  • Learn in order. Tricks 1–5 are the alphabet; everything after is words made from those letters. Triumph (#13) uses #2 and #5.
  • Practice in front of a mirror — or better, film yourself on your phone. You'll spot flashes you'd never feel.
  • 15 focused minutes beats 2 distracted hours. Drill one move until it's boring, then it's ready.
  • Never stare at the secret. Look at the spectator, not your hands, during every secret move. Your eyes tell them where the trick is.
  • Perform early. A trick that's 80% smooth for a real person teaches you more than a week of solo practice.

Foundations — the five moves everything else is built on

1Key Card Principle

Easy

Teaches: the single most useful secret in card magic — knowing a card you were never told.

  1. Casually glimpse and memorize the bottom card of the deck while chatting or squaring up. That's your key — know it cold.
  2. Have the spectator pick a card, show it around, and place it on top of the deck.
  3. Cut the deck once, genuinely, about halfway. Their card is now sitting directly below your key card.
  4. To reveal: spread the deck face-up, spot your key, and name the card right beneath it. Or deal cards face-up one by one — the card after your key is theirs.
Why it works: the cut buries their card under the one card you already know. You'll use this principle inside half the tricks on this page.

📖 Illustrated guide: the key card is simple enough that most teachers cover it on video — but the full treatment is Chapter 6 of Royal Road to Card Magic, and the complete illustrated book (free PDF) is worth downloading regardless.

2Overhand Shuffle Control

Easy

Teaches: controlling a card to the top or bottom while the shuffle looks completely fair.

  1. To the bottom: their card starts on top. Hold the deck in your left hand; lift the top half with your right hand. With your left thumb, peel small packets off the top of the right-hand packet into your left hand until the right hand is empty. The first card peeled — theirs — ends up on the bottom of the reassembled deck.
  2. To keep it there: on later shuffles, always leave the bottom ~10 cards in your left hand — lift packets only from the top, peel, and drop back on top. The bottom stock (and their card) never moves.
  3. The injog (your first marker): when you want to mark a spot, let the final packet land sticking out ~1 cm toward you instead of squared. That protruding packet is a bookmark — later you can cut exactly to it.
The rule to internalize: one honest overhand shuffle sends the top card to the bottom. Once you feel that in your hands, you own the most-used control in magic.

📖 Illustrated guide: The Overhand Shuffle (The Spruce Crafts — photo-illustrated grip and packet work). Bonus reference for the injog: falseshuffle.com's injog page with demo video.

3Hindu Shuffle & Force

Easy

Teaches: a shuffle that looks chaotic but keeps one card exactly where you want it — plus your first force.

  1. Place your force card on top of the deck.
  2. Hold the deck face-down in your left hand, thumb along the left edge. Your right hand comes over the top — thumb at the inner end, fingers at the outer — and pinches off the top portion, pulling it to the right.
  3. With your left thumb, strip small packets off the top of the right-hand packet back into your left hand. That stripping rhythm is the Hindu shuffle. Your right hand keeps hold of the bottom stub throughout.
  4. The force: the bottom card of the right-hand packet never changes during the shuffle — it's always your force card. Have the spectator say "stop" anytime, then tilt the right-hand packet to show its bottom card. That's their "free choice."
  5. Complete the shuffle by dropping the right-hand stub on top — and as you square up, hold a left pinky break exactly where the stub lands. Your force card is right at that break: cut to it whenever you're ready for the reveal.
Why it fools: the spectator watches cards flying and believes the stop selected something random. The bottom card of that packet was decided before you started.

4Double Lift

Easy–Medium

Teaches: the core sleight. Two cards handled as one — the engine inside Ambitious Card, Two-Card Monte, and a hundred others.

  1. As you square the deck, let your left pinky hold a tiny break under the top two cards.
  2. Right hand takes the double from above by the ends — thumb at the inner end, fingers at the outer — lifting both cards as one.
  3. Keep the two cards perfectly squared. A split edge is the only tell — if you see daylight between them, so will everyone.
  4. Turn it over like a book page onto the deck to display it, then turn it back face-down the same way.
  5. The deck's top card is now secretly the second card — deal it, or leave it there for a routine.
Practice drill: push the top two cards off as one with your left thumb, over and over, until a double feels identical to a single. Then practice the turnover in a mirror.

📖 Illustrated guide: two photographed methods (push-off and thumb-slide) in "Unshuffled Card Trick Techniques" (Scribd — login may be required). Follow-up once it's smooth: Alex Pandrea's "The BEST Double Lifts in Magic".

5False Cut

Easy

Teaches: looking scrupulously fair while changing absolutely nothing.

  1. Hold the deck in your right hand. With your left hand, swing-cut the top half off (take it).
  2. Complete the cut: place the right-hand half on top of the left-hand half. That's one genuine cut — top and bottom halves have swapped.
  3. Do the exact same thing again. Two identical half-cuts restore the original order completely.
  4. Do it casually, mid-sentence, exactly the way you'd cut before any trick. Two cuts looks more fair than one — that's the joke, and the power.
Where you'll use it: after any control (#2) or force (#3, #7), a false cut "proves" the deck is mixed while your setup stays intact.

📖 Illustrated guide: False Shuffles, Cuts & Forces (illustrated PDF) — diagrams with arrows for several false cuts including the Charlier.

The Moves — four weapons-grade sleights

6The Glide

Medium

Teaches: stealing the bottom card while everyone watches the bottom of the deck.

  1. Hold the deck face-down in your left hand. Tilt it so the spectator clearly sees the bottom card — "remember this card."
  2. Your right hand comes over the deck from above: thumb at the inner end, fingers at the outer.
  3. As the right hand slides the deck slightly forward, your left fingertips secretly pull the bottom card back about a centimeter — it glides against the deck and stays behind in your left hand.
  4. The right hand lifts away with the deck. The bottom card never left your left hand.
  5. Classic payoff: show the bottom of the right-hand deck — their card has vanished. (Casually drop the retained card to your side or pocket it as you gesture.)
The psychology: the audience just saw the bottom card a second ago. Their brain files it as "still there" — which is exactly why the steal works.

📖 Illustrated guide: the Glide is taught with 120+ diagrams in Royal Road to Card Magic (free PDF), Chapter 1 — still the best diagrammed source on the open web.

7Riffle Force

Medium

Teaches: forcing a card with psychology — plus the secret "slip" that makes it work.

  1. Place your force card on top of the deck.
  2. Hold the deck in your left hand. With your right thumb, riffle down the outer edge — the spectator says "stop" whenever they like.
  3. Freeze at the break. Your right hand lifts the upper packet at the stop point — and as it lifts, your left thumb secretly drags the top card (your force card) back so it stays on the lower packet. Keep the motion tiny and covered by the right hand.
  4. Show the top card of the lower packet: "the card you stopped at." It's your force card.
Why it fools: the spectator believes the stop selected the card. The stop selected nothing — the slip did. Their sense of free choice is the whole trick.

8Elmsley Count

Medium

Teaches: the false count behind a hundred packet tricks — showing four cards while hiding one.

  1. Hold 4 cards face-down in your left hand.
  2. With your right hand, take the cards one at a time from the top, counting aloud: "1, 2, 3, 4."
  3. The secret: cards 2 and 4 are taken under the right-hand packet instead of on top — the right hand dips slightly to receive each one beneath.
  4. Keep the rhythm perfectly even. The metronome-steady "1-2-3-4" is what sells the illusion that all four were counted fairly.
  5. The audience sees four cards counted; one card never shows its face. Classic demo: count four blue-backed cards "fairly," then spread them to reveal one has a red back — it was hidden the entire time.
Common mistake: rushing counts 2 and 4. If the rhythm breaks, the eye catches the dip. Slow is smooth, smooth is invisible.

9Snap Change

Medium–Hard

Teaches: a visual color change — one card visibly becomes another in a snap.

  1. Hold a small packet face-up in your left hand. Your right hand comes over from above.
  2. Press your right middle and ring fingertips onto the face of the top card, near its top edge.
  3. In one sharp snapping motion, drag the top card back toward you — it flips face-down under cover of your right hand — revealing the card beneath. The snap sound plus the speed sells the "visual change."
  4. Commit fully to the motion. Hesitation is the only thing that exposes it.
Angles matter: the Snap Change is devastating head-on and weak from the sides — perform it for someone directly in front of you, and keep the packet low so wandering eyes can't get around it.

📖 Illustrated guide: "Card Snap Change" (GoodTricks — written grip and motion breakdown). Honest note: this one is genuinely best learned from video; the 52Kards tutorial above is the primary path.

Routines — putting it all together

1021-Card Trick

Easy · self-working

Teaches: mathematical magic — a trick with zero sleight of hand that still destroys laypeople.

  1. Deal 21 cards face-up into 3 columns of 7, dealing across row by row.
  2. The spectator memorizes one card and tells you which column it's in.
  3. Gather the columns so their column ends up in the middle of the packet: pick up one of the other columns, then theirs, then the last.
  4. Deal into 3 columns again. Ask the column again. Again sandwich theirs in the middle.
  5. Repeat once more — three rounds total.
  6. Their card is now exactly the 11th card from the top. Reveal it however you like: deal and count, spell its name, have them name it first for drama.
Why it works: each round narrows the card's possible position; three rounds pin it to the exact middle of the packet. The dealing does the "sleight" for you.

📖 Illustrated guide: "21-Card Magic Trick" (free PDF) — concise, with diagrams of the 3×7 dealing layout and the pile-pickup order that make the math click.

11Two-Card Monte

Easy–Medium

Teaches: a pocket-sized miracle — and that presentation is 90% of magic.

  1. Two cards — say the Ace of Spades and the King of Hearts. Show both faces: "Watch the Ace."
  2. Turn the two-card packet face-down as one block, exactly like a double lift (#4). This is the entire secret: the order never changes.
  3. Do the "throw" — toss or flip the packet between your hands with a flourish. It looks like a switch. It isn't.
  4. "Where's the Ace — top or bottom?" Whatever they answer, show them they're wrong.
  5. Repeat. The comedy is that they can never win — lean into it.
The real lesson: nothing sneaky happened, yet the spectator is certain something did. Conviction beats complexity — this is David Blaine's handling, the one that fooled a US president on television.

📖 Illustrated guide: none worth linking — this trick lives and dies on timing, so the video above is the way to learn it.

12Ambitious Card

Medium

Teaches: the classic signature routine — their signed card impossibly rises to the top, again and again.

  1. Have a card selected and signed, then control it to the top (#2).
  2. Phase 1: push it face-up into the middle of the deck. Snap your fingers — it's back on top.
  3. Phase 2 — the double lift turnover: show their card with a double lift (#4), turn the double face-down, then take the top card — secretly an indifferent card — and insert it into the middle. They swear their card went in. It never left the top. Reveal.
  4. Phase 3: lose it in the middle for real this time (overhand control, #2), then place the deck in the card box. It still rises to the top.
  5. Finale idea: the card rises while the deck sits in your pocket, or under a handkerchief.
Start with phases 1–2. Two clean phases beat four sloppy ones. Add phases as each one becomes automatic — the routine is modular by design.

13Triumph

Medium

Teaches: the perfect closer — a shuffled-together mess that rights itself, except for their card.

  1. Their signed card is controlled to the top (#2) and placed face-up on the face-down deck — openly, "so we remember it."
  2. Keep it on top with the top-stock control from trick #2: shuffle everything below it.
  3. Now the theater: perform 2–3 sequences that look like you're mixing face-up cards into the face-down deck — overhand shuffles keeping the top stock (#2) plus false cuts (#5), turning a few packets face-up as you go. Their card's packet stays intact throughout. The mixing is the illusion; their card is the only one whose orientation you secretly preserve.
  4. Finale: slowly square the deck, then spread it — every card is face-down except theirs, face-up.
Why it kills as a closer: the audience is certain the deck is hopelessly mixed — they watched it happen. The full Vernon slip-shuffle handling is in the video; the version above is the honest beginner path using tricks #2 and #5 you already own.
