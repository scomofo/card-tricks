How to practice

  • Get a deck that handles well. Bicycle Rider Backs, broken in for a day or two. It's what every video below uses.
  • Go in order. Triumph at the end leans on the overhand control and the false cut from the start.
  • Film yourself. A mirror is kind; your phone isn't. You'll catch every flash you'd never feel.
  • Fifteen focused minutes beats two hours of fiddling while the TV's on.
  • When the secret happens, look at the person, not your hands. Eyes tell the audience where the trick lives.
  • Perform early. A trick that's merely smooth for a real person teaches you more than another week alone.

Key Card Principle

Easy

The most useful secret in card magic: knowing a card nobody told you.

  1. While you're chatting or squaring the deck, glimpse the bottom card and memorize it. That's your key. Know it cold enough to recognize it without thinking.
  2. Someone picks a card, shows it around, and puts it back on top of the deck.
  3. Cut the deck once, genuinely, somewhere near the middle. Their card is now sitting directly underneath your key card.
  4. Spread the deck face up, find your key, and name the card below it. Or deal cards over one by one — the one after your key is theirs.

You'll use this inside half the tricks here. It's not really a trick, it's a piece of grammar.

Overhand Shuffle Control

Easy

Keep a card exactly where you want it while the shuffle looks honest.

  1. To the bottom. Their card starts on top. Deck in your left hand, lift the top half with your right. Left thumb peels small packets off the top of the right-hand packet into your left hand, until the right hand is empty. The first card peeled was theirs, so it lands on the bottom of the reassembled deck.
  2. Keeping it there. On later shuffles, always leave the bottom ten or so cards sitting in your left hand. Lift packets only from the top, peel, drop them back on top. The bottom stock never moves, and neither does their card.
  3. The injog. When you want to bookmark a spot, let the last packet land sticking out about a centimeter toward you instead of squared. That little protruding step is a marker — later you can cut to it exactly.

The thing to feel in your hands: one honest overhand shuffle moves the top card to the bottom. Once that's muscle memory, you own the most-used control in magic.

Hindu Shuffle & Force

Easy

A shuffle that looks like chaos and a force hiding inside it.

  1. Your force card goes on top of the deck.
  2. Deck face down in your left hand, thumb along the left edge. Right hand comes over the top, thumb at the inner end, fingers at the outer, and pinches off the top portion, pulling it right.
  3. Left thumb strips small packets off the top of the right-hand packet back into your left hand. That stripping rhythm is the whole shuffle. Your right hand keeps hold of the bottom stub the entire time.
  4. Here's the force: the bottom card of the right-hand packet never changes through the shuffle. It's always your force card. Have them say stop whenever they want, then tilt the packet to show its bottom card. Free choice, apparently.
  5. Drop the stub on top to finish, and as you square up, keep a left pinky break right where the stub landed. Your card is sitting at that break — cut to it whenever the reveal calls for it.

They watched cards flying everywhere and they're sure the stop chose something random. The stop chose nothing. That's the entire trick.

Double Lift

Easy–Medium

Two cards handled as one. This is the engine inside half of card magic.

  1. As you square the deck, let your left pinky hold a tiny break under the top two cards.
  2. Right hand takes the double from above by the ends — thumb inner end, fingers outer — lifting both as a single card.
  3. Keep them perfectly squared. A split edge is the only tell there is. If you can see daylight between them, so can everyone.
  4. Turn it over like a book page to show the face, then turn it back the same way.
  5. The top card of the deck is now secretly the second card. Deal it, or leave it there for whatever comes next.

Drill: push the top two off as one with your left thumb, again and again, until a double feels exactly like a single. Then practice the turnover in a mirror.

False Cut

Easy

Look scrupulously fair while changing nothing at all.

  1. Deck in your right hand. Left hand swing-cuts the top half off.
  2. Finish the cut: set the right-hand half on top of the left-hand half. One genuine cut — the halves have swapped.
  3. Now do the exact same thing again. Two identical half-cuts put everything back where it started.
  4. Do it mid-sentence, the way you'd cut before any trick. Two cuts somehow looks more honest than one, which is the whole joke.

Use it after any control or force on this page. It "proves" the deck is mixed while your setup sits there untouched.

Classic Force

Easy–Medium

The boldest force in the book. No shuffle, no setup — just timing and nerve.

  1. Your force card on top of the deck.
  2. Deck face-down in your left hand. Tell them you're going to riffle through the cards and they should just touch the back of one — not take it, just touch.
  3. Right thumb starts riffling down the outer edge, extending the deck toward their waiting hand. Watch their hand, not the cards.
  4. Time it: slow the riffle so the instant their finger commits, the card beneath it is the top card — your force. Stop the moment they touch.
  5. Have them take the card they touched and look at it.

This runs on timing, not mechanics, which is exactly why it looks so fair — there genuinely is nothing to see. And when it misses (it will, sometimes): smile, have them look at the card anyway, and move on. A shrugged-off miss is invisible; a panicked one is the whole trick.

The Palm

Medium

The first real vanish. A card leaves the deck, and your hand is empty — except it isn't.

  1. The card to vanish goes on top. Deck in left-hand dealing grip.
  2. Right hand comes over to square the deck — thumb at the inner end, fingers at the outer.
  3. As the right hand lifts away, it presses down gently and carries the top card with it. The card bows slightly into the palm, clipped by pressure at the base of the fingers.
  4. Keep the hand moving and natural — fingers softly curled, never flat and frozen. A hand in motion never gets questioned.
  5. To bring the card back, reverse the action under cover of squaring the deck again.

Palming is ninety percent acting. The hand looks empty because you behave as if it is. Flash yourself in the mirror on purpose — learning what the bad angles look like is how you learn the good ones.

The Peek

Easy

Learn a card without anyone knowing you looked. The quietest skill in magic.

  1. The bottom peek. Deck face-down in your left hand. As you gesture or chat, tilt the deck a fraction toward yourself and glimpse the bottom card's index. One glance is enough — staring is what gets caught.
  2. The top peek. In dealing grip, your left thumb pushes the top card down and outward a hair, buckling it just enough to sight the index. Cover it by tapping the deck on the table to "square" it.
  3. The riffle peek. Riffle up the back of the deck with your right thumb, lifting the top card just high enough to sight its index.
  4. The rule for all three: your eyes do the work while your mouth keeps talking. Nobody watches a person who's mid-sentence.

A peek isn't a move, it's a moment. The smaller you make it, the more invisible it becomes.

Charlier Cut

Easy

Cut the deck with one hand. Pure flourish — no secret, all style.

  1. Deck in your left hand, held at the fingertips: thumb on one long edge, middle and ring fingers on the other.
  2. Let the bottom half drop into your palm — it falls and pivots against your thumb.
  3. Your index finger pushes the top half forward; it swings down and lands on top of the bottom half. The halves have swapped, one-handed.
  4. Do it again to cut straight back. Clean mechanics first — speed arrives on its own.

This fools no one and impresses everyone. Flourishes are the salt of card magic: a pinch improves everything, a handful ruins the dish.

The Glide

Medium

Steal the bottom card while everyone is looking right at the bottom of the deck.

  1. Deck face down in your left hand. Tilt it so they clearly see the bottom card. "Remember this one."
  2. Right hand comes over from above — thumb inner end, fingers outer.
  3. As the right hand slides the deck forward a touch, your left fingertips pull the bottom card back about a centimeter. It glides along the deck and stays behind in your left hand.
  4. Right hand lifts away with the deck. The bottom card never left your left hand.
  5. Show the bottom of the right-hand deck: their card is gone. Let the retained card fall casually to your side as you gesture.

They saw that card one second ago, so their brain already filed it as "still there." That's what you're stealing — the assumption, not the card.

Riffle Force

Medium

Force a card with psychology — and one small secret slip.

  1. Force card on top.
  2. Deck in your left hand. Right thumb riffles down the outer edge; they say stop whenever they feel like it.
  3. Freeze at the break. Right hand lifts the upper packet at the stop point — and as it lifts, your left thumb drags the top card back so it stays on the lower packet. Small motion, covered by the right hand.
  4. Show the top card of the lower packet: "the card you stopped at." It was never in doubt.

They'll swear the stop chose the card. Protect that belief — it's doing most of the work for you.

Elmsley Count

Medium

Count four cards fairly while one of them never shows its face.

  1. Four cards face down in your left hand.
  2. Right hand takes them one at a time off the top, counting out loud: one, two, three, four.
  3. The second and fourth cards are taken under the right-hand packet instead of on top — the right hand dips a fraction to receive each one beneath.
  4. Keep the rhythm dead even. A metronome "1-2-3-4" is what sells it; any hurry on two and four and the eye catches the dip.
  5. Try it as a trick: count four blue-backed cards "fairly," then spread them — one has a red back. It was hidden the whole time.

Slow is smooth, and smooth is invisible. If it feels too slow to you, it's probably just right for them.

Snap Change

Medium–Hard

One card visibly becomes another, in a snap.

  1. Small packet face up in your left hand. Right hand comes over from above.
  2. Right middle and ring fingertips press on the face of the top card, near its top edge.
  3. One sharp motion: drag the top card back toward you. It flips face-down under cover of your hand, and the card beneath is revealed. The snap of the fingers plus the speed is what reads as a visual change.
  4. Commit to it completely. The only thing that exposes this move is hesitation.

Play it to someone directly in front of you, packet held low. It's devastating head-on and ordinary from the side — so don't give anyone the side.

Top Change

Medium

One card becomes another in the half-second between your hands. The cleanest switch in magic.

  1. The card they know sits on top of the deck in your left hand. The card you're switching it for is face-down in your right hand.
  2. Bring the right hand over the deck, as if you're simply laying its card on top.
  3. In that single passing motion, two things happen at once: the right hand drops its card onto the deck, and your left thumb slides the original top card into the right hand. Neither hand stops moving.
  4. The right hand continues on its way — gesturing, dropping to your side — carrying the stolen card with it.

Speed isn't the point. The exchange happens in plain sight, covered by the logic of the gesture: of course your hand traveled from here to there. If the gesture makes sense, nobody watches the cards.

The Pass

Hard

The invisible cut. Two halves transpose inside your hands and nobody sees a thing.

  1. Left hand holds the deck in dealing grip, a pinky break separating the top and bottom halves.
  2. Right hand comes over the deck — thumb at the inner end, fingers at the outer — covering the break.
  3. In one motion the hands pivot: the bottom half swings up and over while the top half drops beneath. The packets transpose.
  4. Square up immediately, casually, the way you would after any ordinary cut.

This is graduate school — months, not days. Learn it slow and covered; speed is a byproduct of smoothness, never the goal. Until it's invisible, the overhand control (No. 2) does the same job honestly.

Cross Cut Force

Easy

They cut wherever they want. They still end up with the card you picked.

  1. Place the force card on top of the deck and set the deck on the table.
  2. Ask the spectator to cut: they lift off the top portion and set it beside the rest.
  3. Take the remaining bottom portion and lay it crosswise on top of their packet — "marking the exact spot they cut to."
  4. Chat for a beat so the cross reads as a placeholder, then lift the crosswise packet away.
  5. Have them take the top card of the lower packet: the original top card of the deck — your force. Every step felt like their free choice.

Royal Road teaches the mirror-image version with the force starting on the bottom. The top-card handling above is the common modern one — same psychology, less to remember.

Slip Force

Med

Deal a pile, let them stop you anywhere — the stopped-at card is always the one you planted.

  1. The force card starts on top of the deck.
  2. Deal cards face-down onto the table one at a time, inviting them to say "stop" whenever they like.
  3. On every deal, push the top card forward with the thumb as if to take it — then at the last moment slip it back flush and take the next card instead. The top stock never leaves the deck, and every deal looks identical.
  4. When they say "stop," deal the top card honestly. It's the force card.

The classic routine deals all four aces this way — every "freely stopped at" card is an ace, and the spectator believes the whole pile was dealt at random.

21-Card Trick

Easy · self-working

No sleight of hand at all. The math does the trick, and it still floors people.

  1. Deal 21 cards face up into 3 columns of 7, dealing across row by row.
  2. They memorize a card and tell you which column it's in.
  3. Gather the columns so theirs lands in the middle of the packet: pick up one of the other columns, then theirs, then the last.
  4. Deal three columns again, ask again, sandwich theirs in the middle again.
  5. Once more. Three rounds total.
  6. Their card is now exactly the 11th from the top. Deal, count, and turn it over — or let them name it first for maximum drama.

Each round shrinks where the card can be. Three rounds and there's exactly one place left: the middle. A good first trick to perform — it can't go wrong.

Two-Card Monte

Easy–Medium

Two cards, one impossible game. Fits in a pocket, plays huge.

  1. Ace of Spades, King of Hearts. Show both faces: "Watch the Ace."
  2. Turn the two-card packet face down as one block, just like the double lift. Nothing changed order. That's the whole method.
  3. Now the "throw" — toss the packet between your hands with a little flourish. It looks like a switch. It isn't.
  4. "Top or bottom — where's the Ace?" They're wrong. Whatever they said.
  5. Go again. The bit is that they cannot win, so play it like a game they're losing.

Nothing sneaky happened and they're certain something did. That's the lesson here: conviction outperforms complexity. (This is Blaine's handling — the one that fooled a president on live TV.)

Ambitious Card

Medium

Their signed card keeps rising to the top no matter what you do to it. The classic.

  1. Card selected, signed, controlled to the top.
  2. Push it face up into the middle. Snap your fingers. It's back on top.
  3. Now the double lift turnover: show their card with a double lift, turn the double face down, then take the top card — secretly some other card — and push that into the middle. They watched their card go in. It never left the top.
  4. Lose it in the middle for real this time, put the deck in the card box. It still comes back.
  5. Finale, when you're ready: it rises with the deck in your pocket, or under a handkerchief.

Two clean phases beat four shaky ones. The routine is built to grow — add a phase each week and it'll be a different trick by spring.

Triumph

Medium

The deck gets shuffled face-up into face-down, an unsalvageable mess — and then it isn't. Except for their card.

  1. Their signed card, controlled to the top, placed face-up on the face-down deck. Out in the open: "so we remember it."
  2. Keep it there with the top-stock control from No. 2 — shuffle everything below it.
  3. Now the theater. Do two or three sequences that look like you're mixing face-up cards into the deck — overhand shuffles that keep the top stock, false cuts from No. 5 — turning a few packets face-up as you go. Their card's little packet stays intact through all of it. The mixing is the illusion; the one card whose orientation you protect is the trick.
  4. Square the deck slowly. Spread it. Every card face-down — except theirs, face-up.

They watched the mess happen, which is why the cleanup lands so hard. Vernon's original uses a slip-shuffle for the mixing; the video teaches that handling, and the version above gets you there with moves you already own.

Oil and Water

Medium

Reds and blacks, mixed together — then they separate themselves. Your Elmsley count's favorite routine.

  1. Show three red cards, then three black cards.
  2. Mix them face-down, laying them out alternately: red, black, red, black, red, black. Gather the packet — and in the gathering, let the colors quietly regroup: reds together, blacks together.
  3. Elmsley count the packet, showing "mixed" reds and blacks. The count hides the grouping.
  4. Snap your fingers. Deal the packet into two piles: all three reds, all three blacks. Oil and water.

The audience remembers the mixing and the separation. Nobody remembers the gathering — which is exactly where the trick lives.

Twisting the Aces

Medium–Hard

Four aces, face down. Each twist of the packet turns one face-up. Vernon's masterpiece in miniature.

  1. Four aces face-down, in Clubs–Hearts–Spades–Diamonds order from the top.
  2. Triple turnover — lift three cards as one — to show the Ace of Spades. "The heaviest ace. Most ink." Turn them back face-down.
  3. Give the packet a twist: rotate it 180 degrees in your hand. Elmsley count — the Ace of Hearts has turned face-up.
  4. Twist again. Elmsley count, letting the last card fall underneath: the Ace of Clubs, face-up.
  5. The Ace of Diamonds is the stubborn one. Twist, show the packet still face-down all around — then with one last twist, it's face-up in the middle.

Vernon invented the twisting motion to justify the grip the Elmsley count needs. The choreography is the misdirection: they watch the twist, never the count.

Chicago Opener

Medium

Their card changes the color of its back — then becomes someone else's card entirely. The classic opener.

You need one extra: a duplicate of a card with a different-colored back. Blue deck? Find a red-backed mate.

  1. Setup: the red-backed duplicate on top of the blue deck. Force its blue-backed mate — the Hindu force (No. 3) is perfect here.
  2. They look at their card; it goes back in the middle. Control it to the top.
  3. Double lift: turn over "their card" — its back is red. It changed color inside the deck.
  4. Turn the double back over and deal the top card — the red-backed one — face-down to the table. Their blue-backed card is secretly back on top of the deck.
  5. A second card is selected — any card — and lost in the deck. Control it to the top.
  6. Pick up the tabled red-backed card, place it on the deck, and in the same motion switch it for the top card — your top change (No. 16) earns its keep here. Turn it over: the red-backed card is now their second selection.

Two miracles, one odd-backed card. The first phase makes them gasp; the second makes them suspicious of everything — which is exactly what an opener should do.

Biddle Trick

Medium

Their card vanishes from among five cards and turns up somewhere impossible. Elmer Biddle's classic.

  1. A card is selected, noted, and returned. Control it to the top of the deck.
  2. Deal the top five cards into a face-down packet.
  3. Spread them: "Is your card in here?" It is — they confirm it.
  4. Turn the packet face-up and count the cards one by one into your hand. On the second card, execute the Biddle steal: instead of laying it on the counted pile, it stays back beneath the packet — secretly in your right hand.
  5. "Watch closely." Set the counted cards down. Their card has vanished from among the five.
  6. Reveal it impossibly: face-up in the middle of the deck, or produced from your pocket.

The steal happens while they're busy answering your question. Questions are misdirection you never have to choreograph.

Do As I Do

Easy · self-working

You both do exactly the same thing with your own halves — and find the same card. A duet.

  1. The spectator shuffles, then cuts the deck into two roughly equal piles. They take one; you take the other.
  2. You each shuffle your own pile.
  3. "Take any card from your pile, look at it, memorize it. I'll do the same." While they study theirs, glimpse and remember the bottom card of your pile. Your own "selection" is theater — you don't need it.
  4. "Place your card face-down on top of your pile. I'll do the same."
  5. Place your pile on top of their pile. Your glimpsed key card is now sitting directly above their selection.
  6. They cut the combined deck, burying the pair.
  7. "Find your card." They search their memory; you search for your key card — the card right beneath it is theirs. Pull it out as "your" card.
  8. Turn them over together. They match.

The whole trick is one glimpse, hidden inside a flurry of fair actions. The "do as I do" patter isn't just charming — it keeps their eyes on their own hands.

Card to Pocket

Medium

Their signed card leaves the deck and lands in your pocket. The palm's graduation ceremony.

  1. A card is selected, signed, and controlled to the top.
  2. Palm it (No. 15) under cover of squaring the deck.
  3. Hand them the deck: "Hold this. Your card is gone."
  4. Reach into your pocket — or better, have them reach in — and produce their signed card.
  5. Ring the changes: it travels to their pocket, under the card box, or into your shoe.

The trick isn't the palm — it's everything around it. The more ordinary the moments before and after, the more impossible the middle feels.

Si Stebbins Stack

Easy

A secret number system hiding in plain sight — cut all you want, the order never breaks.

  1. Stack the deck: suits cycle Clubs, Hearts, Spades, Diamonds — CHaSeD — and each value runs 3 higher than the last: A♣, 4♥, 7♠, 10♦, K♣, 3♥ …
  2. Because the sequence loops back on itself, the deck can be cut as many times as anyone likes. It never breaks.
  3. Glimpse one card — the bottom is easiest. Say it's the 7♠.
  4. Add 3 to the value (7→10) and step the suit one place forward in CHaSeD (♠→♦): the next card is the 10♦. Name it.
  5. Run it the other way to name the card above, estimate how many cards were cut off, or spell to any named card.

The work is all in the setup. Once the stack is memorized, every effect it powers looks like genuine mind-reading.

Out of This World

Easy

They sort the reds from the blacks without looking — and you're not allowed to touch the deck.

  1. Beforehand, separate the deck: all 26 reds on top, all 26 blacks beneath. A little casual shuffling patter covers the setup.
  2. They deal the cards face-down into two piles, trusting their gut about which pile each card belongs to.
  3. Your only real job: make sure they finish one pile before starting the other. "Deal about half here, then the rest here."
  4. The first 26 cards dealt are all red; the last 26 are all black. The piles sort themselves.
  5. Spread both piles: one all red, one all black. Take your bow.

Paul Curry's 1942 original had you doing the dealing while they called the colors. The spectator-deals version above is the modern standard — and the stronger one, since your hands never touch the cards.

Fitch Cheney's Five-Card Trick

Easy

Your partner leaves the room. Five random cards later, they name the hidden one.

  1. The spectator picks any five cards. Your partner steps out.
  2. Among any five cards, two must share a suit. Hide one of that pair; lay its mate face-up first — it silently names the suit.
  3. Arrange the remaining three cards left-to-right in a coded order: rank them low, medium, high, and use the six possible orders to encode the numbers 1 through 6.
  4. Going around each suit's values, the hidden card always sits within 6 steps ahead of the suit card. The arrangement says exactly how far to count.
  5. Your partner walks in, reads the suit off the first card and the number off the other three, and names the hidden card cold.

No signals, no stooges, no marked cards — just the pigeonhole principle and an afternoon of practice with a patient partner.

Ten-Card Poker Deal

Easy

They deal. They turn over four kings. You still win.

  1. Secretly stack ten cards so a strict alternating deal gives you a straight flush and them four of a kind. The classic shape: your 5♥ 6♥ 7♥ 8♥ 9♥ against their four kings and a queen.
  2. Deal the first two cards yourself, one to each hand — that locks who gets the odd and even positions.
  3. Let the spectator deal the rest, even face-up. The order can't change, so the hands were fixed before the deal began.
  4. They turn over four kings. You turn over a straight flush.

Dozens of versions exist — Buckley's 1946 original, Lorayne's, Trost's. The stack-and-alternation principle is the constant; the video below may use a different stack, but the engine is the same.

The Clock Trick

Easy

Name any hour. The clock already knows.

  1. Secretly arrange thirteen cards, Ace through King, on top of the deck — in order.
  2. Have the spectator deal twelve of them face-down in a circle like the hours of a clock, starting at 1 o'clock. The thirteenth card — the King — stays in your hand.
  3. Ask them to name any hour from 1 to 12, then flip the card at that position. It always matches: position N received the Nth card of your Ace-to-Queen sequence.
  4. Finish by revealing the thirteenth card: the King, watching over all twelve hours.

Don't confuse this with the other trick called the clock trick, where a controlled card is found using the actual time. Same name, different engine.

Miraskill

Easy

They shuffle, they deal, you never touch the cards — and your prediction is right twice.

  1. Beforehand, quietly pocket four red cards. The spectator genuinely shuffles the remaining 48.
  2. Write a prediction — "your pile will come up four cards short" — and set it face-down where everyone can see it.
  3. They deal the deck into face-up pairs: red-red pairs go in a pile in front of them, black-black pairs in a pile in front of you, mixed pairs are discarded.
  4. The math does the work: mixed pairs eat equal numbers of reds and blacks, so the two piles would always come out even — except you stole four reds. Their pile lands exactly four short, word for word as predicted.
  5. Mid-trick, under cover of the counting, slip the four palmed cards back into the discards. Gather everything: round two predicts — correctly — that both piles match this time.

Stewart James, 1935. A pair-separation prophecy, not a "do as I do" — the prediction does the entertaining, the arithmetic does the work.

Thumb Fan

Med

The classic display — a perfect semicircle of cards from one sweep of the thumb.

  1. Hold the deck face-down in one hand, beveled slightly to the side.
  2. Press the thumb of your other hand onto the top edge and sweep it in a smooth, steady arc.
  3. The cards spread into a semicircle, pivoting around the middle finger of the holding hand — the anchor.
  4. Even pressure is the whole skill: too hard and cards clump, too light and the fan collapses. A new deck fans dramatically easier than a worn one.

Learn it with fresh cards, then learn to close the fan one-handed — that's the flourish's natural finish.

The Dribble

Easy

Cards cascading hand to hand in a waterfall — the foundation flourish.

  1. Hold the deck up in one hand, angled slightly downward, thumb along the top edge.
  2. Ease off thumb pressure just enough that cards escape one by one, falling in a steady waterfall into your waiting other hand.
  3. The skill is constant, light pressure: squeeze and you get bursts, relax too far and clumps drop.
  4. It's the same controlled release as the card spring, pointed at the floor instead of across.

Once it's smooth, the dribble becomes the engine for catches, productions, and a dozen other flourishes.

The Spring

Med

Bend the deck, let go — a stream of cards flying hand to hand.

  1. Grip the deck with the thumb along one long edge and the fingers along the other, then bend the packet into a slight arch. The cards sit under tension.
  2. Aim at your waiting other hand and let the thumb release the cards gradually — they shoot off in a tight, arcing stream.
  3. Start with your hands close together. Distance comes only after the release feels smooth and even.
  4. Control beats force: a relaxed, steady thumb release throws a longer, cleaner spring than muscling it.

The spring and the dribble are the same skill in two directions. Learn the dribble first.

Sybil Cut

Hard

Packets dancing between your fingers — and the deck never actually mixes.

  1. Split the deck into four or five packets.
  2. Pick the packets up, display them, twirl them, and set them down in a flowing choreographed sequence — it looks like the most thorough mixing imaginable.
  3. The sequence is constructed so every packet lands back exactly where it started. It's a false cut: pure choreography, and the deck order never changes.
  4. Created by Chris Kenner, published in Totally Out of Control (1992) — the move that helped launch cardistry.

Learn it slowly, packet by packet, with the tutorial below. Speed is the last thing you add — the sequence has to live in your hands first.
